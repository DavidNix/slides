# Freight fraud: research and an agentic demo for AIA

Research checked October 24, 2026. This brief separates published evidence, staff-reported interests, and our fictional demonstration. The selected demo is documented in [Freight claim review and follow-up](../demo-freight-fraud-review.md).

**Recommendation: use freight claims, with the follow-up cycle as the main attraction.** Give Cowork a small claim folder and ask it to prepare the examiner's work: compare the records, create an evidence spreadsheet, draft targeted requests, process replies, and revise the spreadsheet and case note. The audience sees a task advance across several actions. Fraud indicators supply the story; the useful outcome is a better-organized investigation.

The contact's interest and the Claims team's explicit request make this a stronger choice for this audience than the bail collateral exercise. The staff notes specifically mention freight-load research, lengthy claim documents, and scanning documents for fraud indicators. The SiteX comment describes existing equity analysis; it does not identify a bail complaint. [AIA/ACC staff notes](../ai_usage.md).

**The business, without assuming subject expertise.** A **shipper** needs goods moved. A **broker** arranges the transportation. A **carrier** operates the truck. A **receiver** accepts the delivery. A **claimant** is the person or business requesting payment under the bond. The broker whose obligation the bond backs is called the **principal**.

Federal rules require a property broker to maintain $75,000 of financial security through a BMC-84 surety bond or BMC-85 trust. This concerns the broker's covered obligations arising from transportation arrangements. The posted BMC-84 form ties the surety's obligation to the principal's legal liability and caps aggregate payment at the bond amount. It is not $75,000 for every shipment or the amount the broker pays to buy a bond. [Current rule, 49 CFR 387.307](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-387/subpart-C/section-387.307), [FMCSA's posted BMC-84 form](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/docs/registration/80966/bmc-84-5-19-2017.pdf).

For the demo, the practical question is: **“A trucker delivered a load and says it is owed $4,800. Do the documents connect that debt to the broker named on this bond?”** Delivery, identity, payment history, and legal responsibility are separate questions. Finding a suspicious email does not answer all four.

**The strongest new evidence is directly about bond claims.** In a January 17, 2025 interview, Avalon Risk Management, administrator of the TIA bond program, describes stolen broker credentials leading unpaid carriers to file claims against legitimate brokers. Its claims team works with brokers to establish whether they were involved in the shipments. This is practitioner testimony about an actual bond-claims problem, rather than an inference from cargo-theft headlines. It does not establish the frequency or handling of these cases at AIA. [Avalon/TIA interview](https://www.avalonrisk.com/quest-news/tia-bond-program-offers-added-financial-security-around-fmcsa-requirements).

**ACC's own documents make the proposed review concrete.** ACC provides a dedicated freight broker bond claim entry. Its publicly hosted proof-of-claim form asks about the claimant, broker, amount, pickup, delivery, commodity, weight, and agreed rate. Those fields provide a sensible basis for comparing documents. [ACC claims entry](https://www.alleghenycasualty.com/file-a-claim/), [ACC proof-of-claim form](https://www.alleghenycasualty.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Proof-of-Claim-Form-1.pdf).

The indexed text of ACC's online form additionally lists supporting shipment and contractual records, and describes identity verification intended to mitigate fraudulent misrepresentation. Direct retrieval displayed only the interactive form shell, so the indexed checklist should not be represented as a verified current internal procedure. The exercise uses its own instructions. [ACC online claim form](https://claims.accbonds.com/freight-broker-bmc-84-bond-claim-form/).

**Documented scenarios worth drawing from.** These sources establish different mechanisms; none is presented as an AIA claim.

| Published case | What happened | What it supports in a demo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alexis Castillo-Padilla; sentenced February 24, 2023 | A stolen carrier identity was used to obtain loads. Other carriers delivered them, while the organizer collected payment and did not pay those carriers. DOT OIG reports a 30-month sentence and $239,904 restitution. | Compare the booked carrier with the actual trucker. An unpaid claimant may be a victim. [DOT OIG](https://www.oig.dot.gov/library-item/39390). |
| Lakes and Berkovich; sentencing announcement August 10, 2009 | DOJ describes a load accepted for $3,400 and passed to an actual trucker for $4,000. The organizers collected from the original broker and never paid the trucker. | A promise to pay more than the upstream rate can coexist with a real delivery. The price difference alone still does not prove fraud. [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archive/usao/cac/Pressroom/pr2009/098.html). |
| Rajendra Patel; guilty plea announced August 9, 2019 | Connected shipper and carrier businesses built brokers' trust with actual shipments, then obtained payments using fictitious invoices and false supporting records. The announcement describes roughly $2.6 million owed to five brokers. | An alternative demo can compare claimed shipments with independent shipment records. This was a fraud against brokers, not a reported BMC-84 claim decision. [New Jersey Attorney General](https://www.nj.gov/oag/newsreleases19/pr20190809a.html). |
| Avalon's anonymous cargo claim example; January 18, 2024 | An authorized re-brokering arrangement was followed by theft using false credentials. The described contingent cargo policy excluded the particular loss. | Multiple intermediaries are not enough to diagnose fraud. Cargo coverage also introduces a different question from an unpaid freight charge. [Avalon claims example](https://www.avalonrisk.com/quest-news/claims-corner-dishonest-acts-of-third-parties). |

The age of a prosecution does not make its mechanism hypothetical. For current corroboration, FMCSA's April 2025 guidance identifies mismatched contacts, instructions to use a different carrier's name, and inconsistent driver/vehicle records as warning signs. It recognizes that the broker and hauling carrier can both be victims. [FMCSA fraud guidance](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/mission/help/broker-and-carrier-fraud-and-identity-theft).

The FBI's April 30, 2026 advisory describes compromised accounts, lookalike domains, altered shipping records, and changes to contact information in federal records. A website lookup or familiar email address therefore cannot authenticate a transaction by itself. That advisory concerns strategic cargo theft; it supports the identity-checking mechanism, not a conclusion about bond coverage. [FBI/IC3 advisory](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430).

**Two freight demo options.**

| | Option A: delivered load, conflicting identities | Option B: questionable shipment and payment records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience question | “Who actually hired this trucker?” | “Do these invoices represent distinct, unpaid shipments?” |
| Fictional inputs | Claim, booking export, rate agreement, delivery record, email thread, assignment note | Three invoices, delivery records, an earlier payment register, and correspondence |
| Agent's work | Reconstruct the parties; build evidence and follow-up sheets; draft requests; process replies | Match records across files; identify possible resubmissions or unsupported loads; build a reconciliation workbook; request missing evidence |
| Reveal | Replies corroborate delivery and dispute authorization; a promised payment attachment is absent | A reply explains one duplicate as a resubmission, while evidence for another shipment is still missing |
| Real-world grounding | Direct bond-provider account plus federal identity/re-brokering cases | Prosecuted fictitious-shipment scheme; translation into a bond-claim packet is our design |
| Spreadsheet value | Tracks evidence, requests, responses, and remaining work | Particularly natural for comparing many invoices and payment entries |
| Main teaching risk | Treating an innocent unpaid carrier as the fraudster | Treating a repeated document or wrong attachment as proof of an invented shipment |
| Recommendation | **Choose this for the contact's preferred demo.** | Keep as an alternative or later pilot. |

Option A uses a $4,800 claim from fictional Mesa Hauling against a bond for Aster Freight. Aster's booking export names Cedar Motor at $4,200. Mesa's instructions use a different email domain and tell it to check in using Cedar's name. A financing company's appearance as payee has a documented explanation. The source material therefore contains both material questions and an apparent mismatch the agent should clear.

The follow-up phase is essential: the agent drafts questions to Aster, Cedar, and the receiver. Staged replies then confirm Mesa's physical delivery and dispute the booking's authorization. Aster's reply refers to a remittance attachment that is missing from the folder. The agent must notice the missing evidence, update its work, and draft a precise second request. It must not silently convert “attached” into “verified.”

For Option B, construct a paid invoice appearing again in an intake folder, a second invoice accompanied by the first shipment's delivery record, and an ordinary invoice collected by a financing company. The agent should distinguish a possible repeat submission, a document mismatch, and a supported assignment. A corrected attachment or an accounting delay could explain the first two. Do not present these invented clues as features of the Patel case.

**Is this actually a good use of agentic AI? Yes, for the preparation and follow-up workflow.** A model can read and summarize a pasted packet in an ordinary chat. The stronger demonstration gives it access to files and asks for an outcome that requires a sequence of operations:

1. Inventory the available files and identify which records belong to the claim.
2. Extract the parties, shipment details, amounts, and source references.
3. Compare the accounts and identify questions that would resolve the differences.
4. Create a workbook and individual follow-up drafts as actual files.
5. Read new replies and check whether the referenced attachments exist.
6. Update the workbook and case note, preserving unresolved questions and preparing the next request.

The valuable autonomy is choosing and completing those intermediate steps from one brief. The spreadsheet is the visible work product; the changed question after a reply shows that the agent has used the new information. The demo can show file operations and progress without exposing private reasoning or pretending that it made phone calls.

**Where conventional software is sufficient.** Exact invoice-number matching, arithmetic, reminders, and structured status tracking can be handled by ordinary software. An agent becomes more interesting when the evidence is scattered across narrative emails, differently formatted records, and attachments, or when a new reply changes the appropriate next step. In a stable production process, combine deterministic checks with bounded document interpretation rather than making every step an open-ended AI decision.

**What existing AIA systems change.** AIA describes AIMS, Accolade, BailVision, and other software publicly. This establishes existing infrastructure, not a gap in its capabilities or an available integration. The proposed demo uses exported fictional records. Its business hypothesis is that an agent can reduce the work of reconciling documents and maintaining follow-ups around those systems. That hypothesis needs validation with Claims. [AIA technology overview](https://www.aiasurety.com/insurance-solutions/).

**How common is fraud? Be precise about the denominator.** TIA's April 2025 member survey reported that 22% of respondents lost more than $200,000 to fraud in the preceding six months. That supports material industry concern, not a claim that 22% of shipments or AIA claims are fraudulent. [TIA report announcement](https://news.tianet.org/tia-releases-state-of-fraud-in-the-industry-april-2025-report/).

CargoNet's Q2 2026 analysis recorded 677 reported supply-chain theft incidents in the United States and Canada, down 26% year over year, with estimated losses of $304.6 million. A few very large losses influenced severity. These figures concern theft reports, not the prevalence of false bond claims. There is no need to put a dramatic industry statistic into the eight-minute demonstration. [CargoNet, August 6, 2026](https://www.cargonet.com/news-and-events/cargonet-in-the-media/2026-q2-theft-trends/).

**What this research does not establish.** It does not establish AIA's fraud rate, examiner workload, internal investigation rules, recoveries, integration access, or AI accuracy. The named prosecutions support the mechanisms, not every detail of our composite story. The demo cannot authenticate signatures, prove who controlled a mailbox, decide a disputed contract, or determine bond liability merely by reading the packet.

We did not rely on the full TIA fraud framework's case details: its formerly indexed PDF returned an error and the current public link led to the member resource library. The accessible regulator, enforcement, provider, and claim-form sources above support the recommendation without that document.

**Rehearsal and later validation.** The companion guide includes a seven-file initial folder, three staged replies, prompts, timing, and a presenter answer key. Those materials are ready to rehearse; a live Cowork run and its timing have not been tested here. A useful rehearsal must show accurate source references, recognition of the legitimate assignment, a missing-attachment follow-up, and no unsupported claim decision. For a later real-data pilot, measure examiner time including review, unsupported findings, missed issues, and unnecessary follow-up requests across both ordinary and problematic cases.
