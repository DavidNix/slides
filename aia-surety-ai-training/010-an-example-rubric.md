# An example rubric

Triaging incoming claim emails — the bands and criteria are defined by you:

| Band | Observable criteria | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete | Claim #, amount, and date all stated; documents attached | Route to the examiner queue |
| Incomplete | Any one of those missing | Auto-draft a request-for-items reply |
| Disputed | Conflicting amounts or dates vs. the attachments | Human review — no auto-reply |

- The model classifies each email into a band **and quotes the reason** — "no amount stated in the body" → Incomplete.
- You spot-check ten at a glance — a wrong band is visible immediately.
- The number leadership wants is the count of verified items per band, never a score the model invents.

**Define the bands yourself — the model sorts, you verify, the number is your count.**

## Speaker notes

This is what "define a rubric, classify into bands, and verify" looks like in practice — a pattern to copy for any task, not an AIA procedure.

Walk the table: bands come first, and the criteria are facts a person can check in seconds — is the claim number present, does the amount match the attachment. "Disputed" gets no automated action at all: conflicting numbers go to a human before anything is sent.

Insist on the quoted reason. A bare label cannot be verified; a quoted fact can — and that evidence trail is the same one the verification section demands later.

When leadership wants a number, resist asking the model for a score. Have it classify into these bands, count verified items, and recompute weekly. The measurement is trustworthy because it counts checkable facts — spot-checked — rather than reflecting the model's feel for the question.
