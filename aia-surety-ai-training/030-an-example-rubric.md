# An example rubric

A rubric is a short list of levels, each with a plain description. It works like a teacher's grading sheet.

**Don't ask:** "Rate how urgent this email is, from 1 to 10." You get "7". What does 7 mean? Ask again, and you may get 6.

**Ask instead:** "Pick the level that fits, and quote the words that prove it."

| Level | The email belongs here if it... |
|---|---|
| Urgent | names a deadline in the next 2 business days, or a court date |
| Normal | asks a question but names no deadline |
| No action | only shares information and asks nothing |

**You get:** "Urgent. The email says *'the hearing is this Friday.'*" You can check that in five seconds.

## Speaker notes

"Rubric" is not an everyday word. A teacher writes down what an A paper and a C paper look like, so two teachers grade alike.

The 1-to-10 request has no real scale, so "7" cannot be checked.

The rubric fixes two things:

- You write the levels, so they mean the same thing every time.
- The model quotes the words that prove its pick. You can check a quote in seconds.

For a number, count the levels: "12 urgent, 40 normal, 30 no action, ten of each spot-checked."

The same shape works for complaint severity, file completeness, or tone.

Transition: "Some new models only do this: pick from the choices you wrote."
